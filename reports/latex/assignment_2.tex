\chapter{Assignment 2: Semantic Extraction}
\label{sec:assignment2}

Building on the syntactic pipeline of Assignment~1, this stage extracts
\emph{semantic} information from each clause: named entities that identify the
parties, values, and legal references involved; semantic roles that describe who
does what to whom; and an intent label that captures the functional type of the
clause.

\section{Custom Named Entity Recognition (NER)}
\label{subsec:ner}

\subsection{Entity Schema}

Six domain-specific entity types are defined for legal contract NER:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{PARTY} — contracting parties and organisations
          (e.g.\ \textit{Party A}, \textit{ABC International Co., Ltd.}).
    \item \textbf{MONEY} — monetary amounts with or without currency symbols
          (e.g.\ \textit{USD 10,000}, \textit{\$5,000}).
    \item \textbf{DATE} — absolute and relative date expressions
          (e.g.\ \textit{05 May 2024}, \textit{within 30 days}).
    \item \textbf{RATE} — percentage or per-unit rates
          (e.g.\ \textit{1\% per month}, \textit{2\% per annum}).
    \item \textbf{PENALTY} — penalty clauses and liquidated-damages expressions.
    \item \textbf{LAW} — statutory references, ordinances, and legal codes
          (e.g.\ \textit{Civil Code 2015}).
\end{itemize}

The full BIO label set used during model training is:
\begin{center}
    \texttt{O, B-PARTY, I-PARTY, B-MONEY, I-MONEY, B-DATE, I-DATE,
    B-RATE, I-RATE, B-PENALTY, I-PENALTY, B-LAW, I-LAW}
\end{center}

\subsection{Model Architecture}

The system adopts a \emph{hybrid} design in which a deterministic rule-based
extractor and a fine-tuned Legal-BERT model are executed in parallel for every
clause and their outputs are merged into a single entity list.

\paragraph{Primary system — Rule-based NER.}
The \texttt{RuleBasedNER} module applies hand-crafted regular expressions
tailored to each of the six entity types.  Representative patterns include
monetary expressions such as
\texttt{(USD|VND|EUR|GBP|AUD)\textbackslash s*[\textbackslash d,\textbackslash.]+}
and \texttt{\textbackslash\$\textbackslash s*[\textbackslash d,\textbackslash.]+}
for MONEY; ISO-format, ordinal, natural-language, and parenthesised duration
patterns (e.g.\ \textit{1st day of March, 2024}, \textit{thirty (30) Business
Days}, \textit{within 30 days}) for DATE; case-sensitive role nouns
(\textit{Employer}, \textit{Lessor}, \textit{Service Provider}, \ldots) and
\textit{Party A/B} for PARTY; statutory references such as
\textit{Law No.\ 50/2005/QH11}, \textit{Decree No.\ 13/2023/ND-CP},
\textit{Civil Code}, and \textit{SIAC Rules} for LAW; and keywords such as
\textit{penalty}, \textit{liquidated damages}, \textit{termination fee} for
PENALTY.  Because regex matches on legal contract text are highly precise,
rule-based predictions are treated as the primary system and take precedence
during merging.

\paragraph{Supplementary system — Legal-BERT fine-tuned NER.}
A token-classification head is fine-tuned on top of
\texttt{nlpaueb/legal-bert-base-uncased}~\cite{chalkidis2020legal} using the
HuggingFace \texttt{Trainer} API.  Training data combines a small
hand-annotated set
(\texttt{data/english/sample\_ner\_training.json}) with \emph{silver labels}
auto-generated by running the rule-based extractor over every clause produced
by Assignment~1.  This bootstrapping enables Legal-BERT to learn entity
boundaries on PARTY mentions and long multi-token spans that the regexes do
not cover.

\paragraph{Silver-label denoising.}
A noticeable failure mode of the first iteration was that Legal-BERT, after
training on raw silver labels, produced spurious predictions such as
\textit{``of'' $\rightarrow$ RATE} and \textit{``)'' $\rightarrow$ RATE} at
inference time.  These arose because occasional regex over-matches were being
treated as ground truth during fine-tuning.  Two filters were therefore added
(\texttt{\_is\_valid\_silver\_entity}):
(i) reject entity spans of fewer than two characters,
(ii) reject spans that contain no alphanumeric characters, and
(iii) reject spans whose tokens are all closed-class stopwords
(e.g.\ \textit{of}, \textit{the}, \textit{a}, \textit{to}, \textit{and}, \textit{or}).
The same filter is reapplied to Legal-BERT predictions at inference, as a
defence-in-depth against any residual noise.

\paragraph{Prediction merging.}
At inference time, the rule-based and Legal-BERT predictions are combined by
\texttt{\_merge\_entities}: all rule-based spans are kept first, and a
BERT-predicted span is appended only if it does not overlap any existing span.
This guarantees the deterministic regex precision is never overwritten while
still allowing Legal-BERT to contribute additional entities — particularly
PARTY mentions that fall outside the regex vocabulary.  If the fine-tuned
checkpoint at \texttt{models/ner\_legal\_bert\_v2} cannot be loaded, the
pipeline gracefully degrades to rule-based predictions only.

\section{Semantic Role Labelling (SRL)}
\label{subsec:srl}

\subsection{Approach}

Rather than a dedicated BERT-based SRL model, the system uses a
\emph{dependency-tree-based} approach built on top of spaCy's
\texttt{en\_core\_web\_sm} parser.  For each clause, the \texttt{ROOT} token
(typically the main verb) is identified as the predicate, and semantic roles are
assigned to its dependents by mapping Universal Dependencies labels to
PropBank-style argument roles, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:srl-mapping}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Mapping from Universal Dependencies labels to semantic roles.}
    \label{tab:srl-mapping}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{UD Dependency Label} & \textbf{Semantic Role} & \textbf{Notes} \\
        \midrule
        \texttt{nsubj}                 & Agent      & Active-voice subject \\
        \texttt{agent} + \texttt{pobj} & Agent      & Passive ``by'' phrase \\
        \texttt{nsubjpass}             & Theme      & Passive-voice subject \\
        \texttt{dobj} / \texttt{obj}   & Theme      & Direct object \\
        \texttt{iobj}                  & Recipient  & Indirect object \\
        \texttt{prep} (\textit{to})    & Recipient  & Prepositional recipient \\
        \texttt{prep} (temporal)       & Time       & See list below \\
        \texttt{advcl} + cond.\ mark   & Condition  & Subordinate conditional clause \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Temporal prepositions and condition markers.}
A prepositional phrase headed by the ROOT verb is classified as Time when its
preposition belongs to the set
\{\textit{before}, \textit{after}, \textit{on}, \textit{within},
\textit{by}, \textit{until}, \textit{upon}, \textit{during}\}.
An adverbial clause (\texttt{advcl}) attached to the ROOT is classified as
Condition when its subordinating marker (\texttt{mark}) is one of
\{\textit{if}, \textit{unless}, \textit{provided}, \textit{subject}\}.
After the first pass, two fallback sweeps over the ROOT's children fill in any
Recipient or Time slot that was missed because the relevant preposition was
attached at a deeper level.

\paragraph{Clause filtering.}
The contract corpus produced by Assignment~1 contains many fragments that are
not full sentences — section headings (\textit{``ARTICLE 1 — DEFINITIONS''}),
document titles (\textit{``SOFTWARE SERVICES AGREEMENT''}), and short
appositive definitions of parties (\textit{``TechVision Solutions Pte.\ Ltd.''}).
Running spaCy on these fragments yields a noun ROOT and a meaningless
``predicate'' such as \textit{AGREEMENT} or \textit{Ltd.}\ together with
empty role assignments.  Two preprocessing checks are therefore applied:
\texttt{\_is\_processable} rejects all-uppercase fragments and clauses shorter
than five characters, and the SRL extractor additionally requires at least one
\texttt{VERB} or \texttt{AUX} token in the parsed document.  Clauses failing
either check are returned with empty roles and an empty predicate.

\subsection{Output Format}

Results are written to \texttt{output/srl\_results.json} as a list of objects with
the following structure:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=jsonStyle,
                   caption={SRL output format.},
                   label={lst:srl-json}]
[
  {
    "clause_id": 0,
    "text": "The Employer shall pay the Employee the monthly salary ...",
    "predicate": "pay",
    "roles": {
      "Agent":     "The Employer",
      "Theme":     "the monthly salary",
      "Recipient": "the Employee",
      "Time":      "within 30 days"
    }
  }
]
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{SRL Example}

\noindent\textbf{Input:}
\begin{quote}
    \textit{``The Employer shall pay the Employee the monthly salary within 30 days.''}
\end{quote}

\noindent\textbf{Output:}
\begin{quote}
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
    Predicate: & \textit{pay} \\
    Agent:     & \textit{The Employer} \\
    Theme:     & \textit{the monthly salary} \\
    Recipient: & \textit{the Employee} \\
    Time:      & \textit{within 30 days} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{quote}

The ROOT token \textit{pay} is the predicate.  \textit{The Employer} is the active
nominal subject (\texttt{nsubj}) mapped to Agent; \textit{the monthly salary} is the
direct object (\texttt{dobj}) mapped to Theme; \textit{the Employee} is reached via
the indirect object (\texttt{iobj}) relation mapped to Recipient; and
\textit{within 30 days} is a temporal prepositional phrase mapped to Time.

\section{Intent Classification}
\label{subsec:intent}

\subsection{Task Definition and Label Set}

Intent classification assigns each clause one of four functional intent labels.
For each label, a curated list of regular-expression keyword patterns
(compiled with \texttt{re.IGNORECASE}) is used by the weak labeller.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=3pt]
    \item \textbf{Obligation} — a party is required to perform an action.
          Patterns: \textit{shall} (without \textit{not}), \textit{must}
          (without \textit{not}), \textit{is/are required to},
          \textit{is obligated to}, \textit{hereby agrees to},
          \textit{undertakes to}, \textit{will be responsible}.
    \item \textbf{Prohibition} — an action is forbidden.
          Patterns: \textit{shall not}, \textit{must not},
          \textit{is/are prohibited}, \textit{is forbidden},
          \textit{not permitted}, \textit{no Party shall},
          \textit{shall refrain}.
    \item \textbf{Right} — a party is entitled to perform an action.
          Patterns: \textit{may}, \textit{is/are entitled to},
          \textit{has/have the right}, \textit{can},
          \textit{at its/their discretion}, \textit{has the option}.
    \item \textbf{Termination Condition} — the contract terminates upon an event.
          Patterns: \textit{terminat\dots}, \textit{expir\dots},
          \textit{upon breach},
          \textit{in the event of default/breach/non-payment},
          \textit{shall be void}, \textit{null and void},
          \textit{early termination}, \textit{withdrawn},
          \textit{dissolution}.
\end{itemize}

Because several patterns can co-occur within a single clause (e.g.\ a
\textit{shall not} prohibition that also mentions \textit{terminate}), a
priority order
\textbf{Prohibition $>$ Termination Condition $>$ Right $>$ Obligation}
is enforced during weak labelling: the highest-priority intent whose pattern
matches is selected.  Clauses that match no pattern are labelled
\texttt{Unknown} and removed before supervised training.

\subsection{Model 1 — TF-IDF + Logistic Regression}

Training labels are generated by the keyword-regex weak labeller described
above.  Clauses labelled \texttt{Unknown} are discarded, and the remaining
labelled examples are used to fit a scikit-learn \texttt{Pipeline} that
chains a TF-IDF vectoriser to a multinomial Logistic Regression classifier.
Table~\ref{tab:tfidf-config} summarises the configuration.

\subsection{Model 2 — Fine-tuned BERT}

The second classifier fine-tunes \texttt{bert-base-uncased} as a sequence
classifier using \texttt{AutoModelForSequenceClassification}.  At inference
time, a softmax over the logits yields a confidence score that is reported
alongside the predicted label.

\paragraph{Class-imbalance handling.}
On the contract corpus, the weak labeller produces a heavily skewed
distribution: \emph{Obligation} clauses dominate ($\sim$83\%) while
\emph{Right} and \emph{Termination Condition} together account for less
than 5\% of the labelled set.  A plain cross-entropy loss therefore drives
the model to a trivial majority-class predictor that ignores the minority
intents entirely.  To counter this, training uses a class-weighted
cross-entropy loss
\[
\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{i} w_{y_i}\,\log p_{\theta}(y_i \mid x_i),
\qquad
w_c = \frac{N}{C \cdot n_c}
\]
where $N$ is the total number of labelled clauses, $C$ the number of
classes, and $n_c$ the count of class $c$.  The weights are computed once
from the training labels and registered on the same device as the model
inside a custom \texttt{\_WeightedTrainer} subclass that overrides
\texttt{compute\_loss}.  In addition, an
\texttt{EarlyStoppingCallback(patience=2)} watches the macro-F1 on the
held-out split, and the best checkpoint is restored at the end of training.

settings.

\section{Results on the Contract Corpus}
\label{sec:a2-results}

This section reports the actual outputs of the pipeline on the 170 clauses
produced by Assignment~1 from a Software Services Agreement.  These numbers
were obtained after applying the silver-label denoising, SRL clause filtering,
and class-weighted loss described in the preceding sections.

\subsection{NER results}

The hybrid extractor identified \textbf{249 entities across 114 of 170
clauses} (67\% clause coverage).  Crucially, the spurious single-token spans
that appeared in the first iteration (\textit{``of'' $\rightarrow$ RATE},
\textit{``)'' $\rightarrow$ RATE}) are now \emph{entirely absent}, confirming
that the \texttt{\_is\_valid\_silver\_entity} filter is effective both during
training-set construction and at inference time.
The entity-type distribution is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:ner-results}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{NER entity-type distribution on the contract corpus.}
    \label{tab:ner-results}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Label} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Representative span} \\
        \midrule
        PARTY    & 171 & \textit{Party A}, \textit{Service Provider}, \textit{the Parties} \\
        DATE     &  35 & \textit{1st day of March, 2024}, \textit{within thirty (30) days} \\
        LAW      &  17 & \textit{Law No.\ 50/2005/QH11}, \textit{Circular No.\ 05/2017/TT-BTC} \\
        MONEY    &  14 & \textit{\$150,000 USD}, \textit{\$2,000,000 USD} \\
        PENALTY  &  10 & \textit{late payment penalty}, \textit{liquidated damages} \\
        RATE     &   2 & \textit{1.5\%} \\
        \midrule
        \textbf{Total} & \textbf{249} & \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The PARTY count dominates because contracting parties are referenced in almost
every operational clause, which matches the expected linguistic profile of a
bilateral services agreement.  The relatively low RATE count reflects that
this particular contract contains only one explicit interest-rate clause
(\textit{1.5\% per month}); the rule-based pattern is correctly conservative
and does not match unrelated percentages from text such as ``100\%
ownership''.

\subsection{SRL results}

After clause-level preprocessing, 36 clauses (21\%) are correctly classified
as non-sentential fragments (titles, article headers, bare entity
references) and receive an empty predicate.  The remaining 134 clauses
produce a verb predicate; of these, 122 yield at least one extracted role,
and only 12 verbal clauses receive an empty role set.  The aggregate role
distribution is shown in Table~\ref{tab:srl-results}.

    \centering
    \caption{SRL role distribution on the contract corpus (134 verbal clauses).}
    \label{tab:srl-results}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Role} & \textbf{Count} \\
        \midrule
        Agent     & 92 \\
        Theme     & 90 \\
        Recipient & 18 \\
        Condition & 19 \\
        Time      & 14 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

No predicate is now incorrectly assigned to a noun token: previous spurious
predicates such as \textit{AGREEMENT}, \textit{Ltd.}, \textit{Corp.}, and
\textit{definition} no longer appear.

\subsection{Intent classification results}

Class-aware training has clearly broadened the predicted label distribution
compared with the first iteration, in which BERT collapsed to predicting
\emph{Obligation} on 166 of 170 clauses.  The updated label counts produced
by the two models are reported in Table~\ref{tab:intent-results}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Predicted intent distribution (170 clauses).}
    \label{tab:intent-results}
    \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Label} & \textbf{TF-IDF} & \textbf{BERT} \\
        \midrule
        Obligation             & 113 &  99 \\
        Prohibition            &  29 &   3 \\
        Right                  &  15 &   5 \\
        Termination Condition  &  13 &  63 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{TF-IDF + class-balanced LR is the stronger model on this corpus.}
The class-weighted Logistic Regression produces a distribution that is
consistent with manual inspection: \emph{Obligation} clauses dominate but the
minority intents (\emph{Right}, \emph{Termination}, \emph{Prohibition}) are
no longer crushed to single-digit counts.

\paragraph{BERT remains under-confident.}
Although BERT now predicts all four classes, the mean softmax confidence
across the corpus is only 0.355 and \textbf{every} clause receives a
confidence below 0.5.  Manual inspection shows that BERT mislabels several
clearly obligatory clauses as \emph{Termination Condition} (63 BERT
predictions versus only 13 from TF-IDF for this label).
Two factors explain this behaviour and are not deficiencies of the model
itself:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Tiny training set.}  After discarding \texttt{Unknown}
          clauses, the supervised training set contains only $\sim$150
          examples.  Fine-tuning a 110M-parameter BERT model on this volume
          typically requires either external augmentation or upstream
          domain pre-training.
    \item \textbf{Severe class imbalance.}  Even after weighting, the
          training signal for \emph{Right} (3 raw examples) and
          \emph{Termination Condition} (3 raw examples) is too weak for
          stable representation learning.
\end{itemize}
For deployment on this corpus, the recommendation is therefore to use the
TF-IDF model as the primary classifier and to consult BERT only as a
soft tie-breaker when the TF-IDF prediction is near-uniform across classes.
